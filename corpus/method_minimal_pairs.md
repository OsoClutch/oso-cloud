# Teaching the sounds that change meaning

A minimal pair is two words that differ in exactly one sound and mean different
things. They matter because an adult learner does not hear the contrasts their
first language does not use — the sounds are physically audible but not
distinguished, so they are reproduced as the nearest familiar sound. Training
has to make the contrast matter before production improves.

Some contrasts that reliably defeat learners: Japanese long and short vowels and
the r/l region for English speakers; Spanish rolled and tapped r; tone in
Mandarin; vowel length in Dutch and German; nasal vowels in French.

## What this means for the sounds stage

- Teach the **inventory** first when the script is unfamiliar: one card per
  character or sound, answered aloud.
- Then teach the **contrasts** that change meaning, as deliberate pairs, so the
  learner hears what is at stake.
- Keep the spoken answer to a syllable or two. Speech recognisers grade single
  syllables badly, so a sounds card is at the edge of what can be graded
  automatically — which is why an honest "unclear" verdict matters more here
  than anywhere else.

## Consequence for generated decks

- A `sounds` deck is the only deck type where a card's answer may be a single
  syllable.
- For a language written in the Latin alphabet and already familiar to the
  learner, the `sounds` stage is not the alphabet — it is the handful of sounds
  and spelling-to-sound rules that differ from the learner's own language.
- Always provide the romaji or phonetic field. The grader compares pronunciation,
  not orthography.
